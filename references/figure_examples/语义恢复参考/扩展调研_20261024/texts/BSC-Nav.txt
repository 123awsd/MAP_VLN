Spatial cognition enables adaptive goal-directed behavior through struc-
tured internal models of space. Robust biological systems consolidate spa-
tial knowledge into three interconnected forms: landmarks for salient cues,
route knowledge for movement trajectories, and survey knowledge for map-
like representations. While recent advances in multi-modal large language
models (MLLMs) have enabled visual-language reasoning in embodied
agents, these efforts lack structured spatial memory and instead operate
reactively, limiting their generalization and adaptability in complex real-
world environments. Here we present Brain-inspired Spatial Cognition for
Navigation (BSC-Nav), a uniﬁed framework for constructing and leveraging
structured spatial memory in embodied agents. BSC-Nav builds allocentric
cognitive maps from egocentric trajectories and contextual cues, and
dynamically retrieves spatial knowledge aligned with semantic goals. Inte-
grated with powerful MLLMs, BSC-Nav achieves state-of-the-art efﬁcacy and
efﬁciency across diverse embodied navigation tasks (e.g., improving success
weighted by path length from 17.6% to 44.9% in instance-level navigation and
from 42.7% to 53.1% in zero-shot long-horizon instruction-following), while
also supporting versatile embodied behaviors in the real physical world.
These results highlight a scalable path toward general-purpose spatial
intelligence.
Spatial cognition, the ability to acquire, organize, exploit, and update
knowledge about external space, is fundamental to both human beings
and artiﬁcialintelligence (AI)1,2. It underlies not only sensorimotor skills
such as navigation and manipulation but also supports higher-level
cognitive functions, including abstraction, planning, and reasoning. In
humans, generalizable spatial representations support interpretation
of sensory input, anticipation of future events, and ﬂexible adaptation
to changing environments, from brewing coffee in a familiar kitchen to
navigating an unfamiliar city3–5. Given its broad relevance in embodied
interaction with the real physical world, spatial cognition has emerged
as a central theme across disciplines, driving advances in robotics6,
urban simulation7, and planetary-scale modeling8, while increasingly
recognized as a foundational component of artiﬁcial general intelli-
gence (AGI)3,9,10.
Despite the rapid progress in multi-modal large language mod-
els (MLLMs) and growing efforts to equip embodied agents with
visual-language reasoning11–13, current artiﬁcial systems remain fun-
damentally limited in large-scale spatial cognition14, especially in
tasks requiring long-horizon navigation and mobile manipulation. A
central bottleneck lies in the lack of structured spatial memory15–17, a
mechanism for persistently encoding, organizing, and retrieving
spatial knowledge about the environment. Most existing methods,
whether based on end-to-end reinforcement learning18–20 or modular
pipelines with powerful MLLMs21,22, process observations in a reactive

space, agents struggle to consolidate coherent spatial representa-

fragmented knowledge, short-sighted planning, and poor general-

Compared to artiﬁcial systems, biological spatial cognition
offers a robust and compelling template. Decades of neuroscience
research revealed that organisms consolidate spatial knowledge into
three distinct yet interconnected forms16,17 (Fig. 1a): landmarks, which
encode stable associations of salient environmental cues to support
localization and contextual understanding23,24; route knowledge,
which captures egocentric movement trajectories between land-
marks for habitual navigation and path integration25; and survey
knowledge, which integrates multiple routes into allocentric, map-
like representations that support ﬂexible inference, shortcut dis-
covery, and detour planning26. These spatial representations are
accessed and coordinated via working memory27, especially visual-
spatial working memory28,29, enabling adaptive retrieval, composi-
tion, and generalization based on task demands and environmental
familiarity.
Motivated by these biological principles, we introduce Brain-
inspired Spatial Cognition for Navigation (BSC-Nav), a uniﬁed frame-
work that instantiates cognitive spatial intelligence in embodied
agents via structured spatial memory (Fig. 1b). BSC-Nav explicitly
constructs spatial knowledge via two synergistic branches: a landmark
memory module, which encodes durable associations between salient
environmental cues and 3D spatial locations to instantiate landmarks;
and a cognitive map module, which accumulates egocentric observa-
tions along movement trajectories as route knowledge and then
organizes the voxelized trajectories into allocentric, map-like repre-
sentations as survey knowledge. Compared to previous embodied
navigation frameworks conﬁned to localized modeling with a single
granularity, such as purely dense grids30,31 or sparse topologies32,33, the
hierarchical spatial architecture of BSC-Nav achieves the decoupling of
spatial knowledge while incorporating targeted adaptive update
mechanisms. This allows spatial knowledge to continuously evolve as
the agent explores external environments, overcoming the limitations
of singular, static spatial representations.
BSC-Nav further incorporates a working memory module that
dynamically retrieves and combines spatial representations from
landmark memory and the cognitive map, thereby aligning semantic
goals with grounded spatial actions. Each component interfaces
seamlessly with large-scale foundation models: visual foundation

Results
We begin by presenting the BSC-Nav framework, which instantiates
brain-inspired structured spatial memory in embodied agents. We
then systematically evaluate its performance across both simulated
and real-world scenarios, highlighting: (i) universal navigation cap-
abilities in foundational tasks; (ii) higher-level spatially aware skills in
instruction-driven visual-language navigation and active embodied
question answering; and (iii) real-world efﬁcacy in navigation and
mobile manipulation.
Construction and exploitation of structured spatial memory in
embodied agents
Structured spatial memory is essential for guiding goal-directed
behavior in complex real-world environments3–5, yet remains largely
absent from current embodied agents. Drawing inspiration from the
robust
spatial
representations
in
biological
systems,
namely,
landmarks23,24, route knowledge25, and survey knowledge26, BSC-Nav
implements a modular architecture that explicitly constructs and
exploits analogous memory structures to achieve spatial cognition
capabilities (Fig. 1b, detailed in Methods).
BSC-Nav comprises two branches that are continuously updated
to accumulate and organize spatial knowledge during environment
exploration. First, a landmark memory module encodes salient envir-
onmental cues as associative triplets comprising spatial coordinates,
semantic categories, and contextual descriptions, consolidating land-
marks of the surroundings. This design yields abstract and sparse
representations that prioritize salient instances, enabling efﬁcient
retrieval and forming a ﬂexible, interpretable scaffold of the external
space (detailed in Methods). Meanwhile, a cognitive map module
accumulates egocentric observations along movement trajectories as
route knowledge, which are then voxelized and organized into per-
sistent allocentric representations as survey knowledge (detailed in
Methods). Motivated by the broad idea in free-energy37,38 and
predictive-processing39,40 accounts, that biological systems update
internal representations in response to mismatches between obser-
vation and expectation, we implement a surprise-driven update strat-
egy
that
selectively
integrates
novel
or
unexpected
spatial
observations. Here, this mechanism refers to a heuristic computational
design, rather than a strict implementation of variational free-energy
optimization41.
To exploit structured spatial memory during task execution, BSC-
Nav further incorporates a working memory module that adaptively
retrieves landmarks, route knowledge, and survey knowledge for goal
localization and trajectory planning (detailed in Methods). Analogous
to visual-spatial working memory in biological systems28,29, this mod-
ule dynamically composes spatial representations based on task
demands and environmental familiarity.
We propose a hierarchical retrieval strategy guided by the com-
plexity of the goal. For simple targets, the agent leverages MLLMs22,42 in
a text-only form to reason over semantic associations and contextual
cues within the landmark memory (Fig. 1c). For more complex or
ambiguous instructions, BSC-Nav activates association-enhanced
retrieval over the cognitive map. In this process, MLLMs enrich the
initial goal descriptions by inferring object-speciﬁc attributes and
scene-level priors, transforming vague commands into semantically
detailed representations. These enriched descriptions are then ren-
dered into imagined visual prototypes using a text-to-image diffusion
model43. The generated images are encoded and matched against
dense visual features stored in the cognitive map to localize candidate
target regions. This hierarchical retrieval strategy allows BSC-Nav to
access complementary forms of spatial memory in response to target
complexities (category-level and instance-level) and modalities (text
and image), supporting precise localization for universal goal-directed
navigation (Fig. 2).
Working memory retrieval often produces multiple candidate
coordinates, each associated with a conﬁdence score and relative
distance. In scenarios with multiple valid targets (e.g., category-level
navigation), conﬁdence scores alone may not reliably indicate cor-
rectness. Rather than greedily selecting the candidate with the highest
conﬁdence, BSC-Nav employs a composite ranking strategy that inte-
grates distance- and conﬁdence-based scores to determine an efﬁ-
cient, adaptive exploration sequence. Candidate targets are then
sequentially visited and veriﬁed using online visual-semantic obser-
vations parsed by the MLLM until the goal is conﬁrmed. A more
detailed illustration of candidate generation, ranking, and veriﬁcation
is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1. Low-level movement policies are
generated using heuristic planning along this sequence. During
execution, MLLMs continuously parse spatial-semantic context from
observations, verify landmark proximity, and conﬁrm goal arrival,
enabling affordance-aware action generation. Upon reaching the
appropriate target locations, agents can invoke additional skills, such
as grasping objects or answering questions, to accomplish more
complex, task-driven objectives (Fig. 1c).
Universal navigation across modalities and granularities
Through brain-inspired spatial cognition that incorporates the three
types of spatial knowledge, BSC-Nav demonstrates strong general-
ization across diverse navigation tasks and consistently outperforms
recent strong baselines in both success rate and navigation efﬁciency.
We conduct systematic evaluations across 8195 episodes in 62
indoor scenes from physically reconstructed environments (MP3D44
and HM3D45 datasets) in the Habitat simulator46, covering four repre-
sentative navigation tasks (Fig. 3): Object-Goal Navigation (OGN)18,47,48,
Open-Vocabulary Object Navigation (OVON)49, Text-Instance Naviga-
tion (TIN)50, and Image-Instance Navigation (IIN)51. In each episode,
agents initialized at random positions execute discrete actions (for-
ward 25 cm, turn left 30∘, turn right 30∘, and stop)52. Following standard
protocols52, navigation succeeds only when agents execute a stop
within 1.0 m of goals. Evaluation metrics include the Success Rate
(SR)53, deﬁned as the fraction of completed episodes for efﬁcacy; and
the Success weighted by Path Length (SPL)53, which scales the binary
success by the ratio of the shortest path distance to the agent’s actual
trajectory length for efﬁciency (detailed in Sec. 4.2). We compare BSC-
Nav with end-to-end methods (PixNav11, DAgRL49, PSL50) and modular
methods that instantiate only analogous landmark memory (VLFM13,
MOD-IIN54, UniGoal33, GOAT36). Figure 3a summarizes the quantitative
benchmark results for category-level and instance-level navigation,

As shown in Fig. 3a, BSC-Nav demonstrates superior spatial gen-
eralization in category-level navigation (OGN and OVON). For common
targets in OGN tasks, BSC-Nav achieves 78.5% SR on HM3D (6 cate-
gories) and 56.5% on MP3D (20 categories with larger areas), sur-
passing the state-of-the-art method UniGoal by 24.0% and 15.5%,
respectively. Unlike UniGoal, which organizes only landmark memory
with abstract goals and scene graphs, BSC-Nav leverages structured
spatial memory and thus obtains signiﬁcant performance gains. In the
more challenging OVON tasks involving 79 everyday objects (e.g.,
“kitchen lower cabinet”), BSC-Nav maintains 40.2 and 38.9% SR on
MP3D’s unseen and seen validation sets, outperforming the supervised
method DAgRL in zero-shot settings. The results of instance-level
navigation (TIN and IIN) further validate BSC-Nav’s robust multi-modal
generalization. In TIN tasks, it nearly doubles the SR compared to
UniGoal and VLFM. In IIN tasks, direct matching of target images with
cognitive map features yields 71.4% SR, outperforming UniGoal
by 11.4%.
Notably, BSC-Nav achieves signiﬁcantly higher navigation efﬁ-
ciency across all tasks compared to baseline methods, as measured by
SPL scores. This improvement stems from our distance- and
conﬁdence-based scoring strategy within the working memory mod-
ule, which enables the agent to plan efﬁcient exploration sequences by
examining only a small number of candidate locations (Supplementary
Fig. 3). Interestingly, OGN and IIN generally reach higher SR and SPL
with fewer explored candidates than OVON and TIN (Supplementary
Fig. 3). We attribute this difference to target ambiguity: OGN and IIN
typically provide stronger semantic or visual grounding, whereas
OVON and especially TIN require broader open-vocabulary reasoning
or ﬁner-grained instance disambiguation, making candidate veriﬁca-
tion more challenging. Besides, performance differences across target
categories likely reﬂect both visual saliency and contextual regularity.
Large and visually distinctive objects such as beds are often easier to
localize and verify because they occupy more stable and semantically
predictable regions, whereas smaller or less distinctive targets can
introduce greater ambiguity during retrieval and stopping decisions. A
representative visualization example is presented in Fig. 3b, including
top-down trajectories, ﬁrst-person observations, and MLLM-assisted
target veriﬁcation. We provide additional examples across various
navigation tasks in Supplementary Movie 1 and Supplementary Fig. 4.
Furthermore, detailed numerical results for all four navigation tasks
are provided in Supplementary Tables 1–3.

Real-world navigation and mobile manipulation
To demonstrate BSC-Nav’s generalization beyond simulation and its
broad applicability in downstream tasks, we deploy it on a custom-
built mobile robotic platform within physical indoor environments.
This evaluation demonstrates how structured spatial memory enables
reliable long-range navigation and integrated manipulation in the real
physical world. The mobile platform (Fig. 5a) features a compact
mechanical design equipped with high-precision motion control, as
well as modular sensory and actuation capabilities. We conduct a total
of 75 navigation episodes in a two-story indoor space (around 200 m2,
Fig. 5b), covering three types of goal-directed tasks: Object-Goal
Navigation (OGN), Text-Instance Navigation (TIN), and Image-Instance
Navigation (IIN), with ﬁve distinct targets per task (Fig. 5c). Each target
is tested over ﬁve trials from randomly sampled starting positions,
with an average path length of 23.4 m.
BSC-Nav demonstrates robust spatial generalization in all real-
world tasks. As shown in Fig. 5d, it achieves at least 3 out of 5 successful
trials per target (SR is deﬁned as reaching within 1.0 m), with IIN per-
forming best, reaching 100% SR on 4 out of 5 targets. Both OGN and
TIN reach 100% SR on two targets and exceed 66.7% SR across all cases.
Even in failure cases, BSC-Nav reliably localizes to semantically plau-
sible regions, demonstrating strong spatial awareness. The distance-
to-goal distribution in Fig. 5e further conﬁrms the precise stop loca-
tion, with the ﬁnal distance below 2.5 m in all cases and tight clustering
between targets, particularly for IIN. Navigation efﬁciency is reﬂected
in a mean velocity of 0.76 m/s with low variance (Fig. 5f), indicating
stable and efﬁcient movement. Representative navigation trajectories
are visualized in Fig. 5g, with complete demonstrations available in
Supplementary Movies 3–7. The trial-level raw data for all 75 real-world
navigation episodes are provided in Supplementary Tables 6–8.
To further demonstrate the advantages of the BSC-Nav archi-
tecture in real-world deployments, we conduct an ablation study on
the Text Instance Navigation (TIN) task, comparing the full BSC-Nav
against a landmark-memory-only variant (i.e., excluding the cognitive
map). The conﬁguration of this variant serves a dual critical purpose:
(i) it acts as a proxy baseline for existing modular methods that model
solely landmark-level spatial knowledge without incorporating dense
visual-spatial representations; and (ii) it provides a controlled setting
to rigorously analyze the speciﬁc contributions of different memory
modules in physical environments. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 5c,
the landmark-only baseline achieved an overall navigation success rate
of merely 52%, substantially lower than that of the full BSC-Nav (78%).
This pronounced performance gap arises because the baseline, strip-
ped of the dense visual-spatial features provided by the cognitive map,
struggles immensely with text-instance targets that necessitate ﬁne-
grained visual disambiguation. Constrained exclusively to category-
level semantic matching, the baseline method is forced to resort to
inefﬁcient sequential exploration across a series of candidate way-
points. Supplementary Fig. 5d visualizes this candidate region
exploration process for the landmark-only variant, and the detailed
quantitative raw data are provided in Supplementary Table 9.
Beyond navigation, BSC-Nav’s structured spatial memory sup-
ports robust mobile manipulation guided by natural language.
Although prior embodied manipulation systems62,63 have shown pro-
mise in static, small-scale environments, they often lack the spatial
generalization necessary for large-scale deployment. BSC-Nav over-
comes this limitation by seamlessly integrating long-horizon naviga-
tion with goal-conditioned manipulation. In our demonstrations,
natural language instructions are interpreted by GPT-464 to generate
waypoint-action sequences. Upon reaching each waypoint, the agent
executes a predeﬁned manipulation primitive, such as grasp, place,
and pour. Representative examples in Figure 6 show both single-step
and multi-step tasks. Notably, the “make breakfast” task (Fig. 6c)
involves identifying and interacting with three spatially distributed,
open-vocabulary objects, emphasizing the critical role of structured
spatial memory in long-horizon reasoning and reliable object
grounding. Full demonstrations are provided in Supplementary
Movies 8–10.
Discussion
This work demonstrates that organizing embodied navigation around
high-level principles inspired by biological spatial cognition can be
effective in practice. By constructing a structured spatial memory
comprising computational counterparts of landmarks, route knowl-
edge, and survey knowledge, BSC-Nav improves navigation perfor-
mance and extends the spatial capabilities that complement the
perceptual and reasoning strengths of modern MLLMs.
Conventional embodied agents often rely on reinforcement
learning or imitation learning, acquiring task-speciﬁc policies through
extensive
trial-and-error
or
teleoperated
demonstrations.
Here,
“embodied” refers to agents that perceive and act in closed-loop
interaction with physical or simulated environments, rather than
explicitly modeling morphology-dependent cognition in the stronger
sense emphasized in embodiment theory65. While effective in con-
trolled settings, prior methods remain fundamentally reactive,
responding to immediate stimuli without persistent or reusable spatial
knowledge. BSC-Nav addresses this limitation by integrating struc-
tured spatial memory with foundation models, enabling a transition
from observation-driven pattern matching to multi-level spatial rea-
soning. BSC-Nav exhibits strong performance across navigation tasks
of varying modalities and granularities, especially in open-vocabulary
and instance-level settings. These results demonstrate how memory-
centric spatial representations enable such agents to decouple plan-
ning from immediate perception, reuse prior experience across tasks,
and translate high-level goals into concrete actions, which we view as
important components of cognitive spatial intelligence. Importantly,
BSC-Nav is not purely retrieval-based but also supports active
exploration by ranking candidate targets, selectively visiting promis-
ing locations, and verifying goals through ongoing interaction with the
environment.

BSC-Nav is particularly suited to goal-directed embodied opera-
tion in partially observable environments, where agents must link
semantic goals to persistent spatial structure, revisit previously seen
regions, disambiguate candidate targets, and coordinate navigation
with downstream interaction. This makes the framework directly
relevant not only to service and assistive robotics, but also to embo-
died question answering and other long-horizon tasks that require
accumulated, queryable, and updateable spatial experience. Extending
BSC-Nav across environmental scales, however, poses distinct chal-
lenges rather than a single scaling problem. In compact environments,
the key issue is representational economy, namely avoiding redundant
memory growth while maintaining efﬁcient retrieval. In larger or more
open-ended environments, the main bottlenecks shift to memory
capacity, map consistency, and retrieval efﬁciency. These observations
highlight scalability as an important next consideration for structured
spatial memory beyond the benchmark settings studied here.
As embodied AI systems advance in perceptual and motor cap-
abilities, it becomes increasingly important to evaluate not only whe-
ther agents can act successfully in physical environments but also how
language and spatial cognition interact to support such behavior. In
cognitive science, growing evidence suggests that language is not
merely a detached symbolic interface but can itself serve as a source of
grounding and a mode of action, while spatial knowledge and map-like
representations
are
in
turn
deeply
entangled
with
language
experience67–69. Our results are broadly consistent with this perspec-
tive. In BSC-Nav, linguistic goals become most effective when they can
be anchored to structured spatial memory, whereas spatial memory
becomes more ﬂexible when it can be queried, abstracted, and veriﬁed
through language-guided reasoning. This interplay suggests that
human-like spatial intelligence may depend not only on building
internal models of space, but also on coupling them tightly with lin-
guistic representations that support abstraction, disambiguation, and
goal speciﬁcation. From this viewpoint, a future embodied Turing test
for spatial cognition70 may need to assess not only navigation accu-
racy, but also whether agents can construct reusable spatial repre-
sentations, reason from sparse observations, and translate natural-
language goals into grounded spatial plans in ways that remain robust
across tasks and environments.
Despite its effectiveness, the present work still has several limita-
tions. First, although BSC-Nav is organized around high-level principles
inspired by biological spatial cognition, it is not intended as a cognitively
faithful model of human cognition. In particular, the language and visual
reasoning components are implemented with modern MLLMs, which
are used primarily as engineering modules for semantic grounding,
visual interpretation, and high-level reasoning, rather than as biologi-
cally or developmentally plausible models of human language proces-
sing. Recent psycholinguistic work has likewise emphasized that strong
linguistic generalizations learned by neural language models do not by
themselves establish cognitively plausible mechanisms, especially for
large-scale models trained far beyond human-like data conditions71.
From this perspective, BSC-Nav shows that structured spatial memory
can provide an effective computational interface between perception,
language, and action, rather than offering a mechanistic account of how
these functions are jointly realized in the brain. Second, while the fra-
mework has clear conceptual implications for memory-centric embo-
died agents, its theoretical contribution remains primarily architectural
and computational. More formal analyses of when and why structured
spatial memory improves generalization, efﬁciency, and language-
guided behavior remain important directions for future work.
Several future directions emerge from this work. Extending BSC-
Nav to dynamic and unstructured environments will require spatial
memory that can be continually revised as scenes evolve over time.
Scaling to larger environments will in turn demand more efﬁcient
memory organization, including hierarchical abstraction, compact
storage, and retrieval with bounded latency. Richer embodied settings
may further beneﬁt from integrating additional sensory streams, such
as depth, audio, or tactile signals, together with uncertainty-aware
updating. Beyond single-agent navigation, future systems should also
support collaborative and multi-agent spatial reasoning, where mem-
ory can be shared, communicated, or jointly reﬁned. More broadly, an
important longer-term direction is to explore architectures in which
memory, perception, and language are developed under stronger
constraints of embodiment, experience, and computational efﬁciency.
Progress along these directions may help narrow the gap between
high-performing engineering systems and more cognitively grounded
models of spatial intelligence.

Goal veriﬁcation and affordance. Upon arriving at a candidate
coordinate, BSC-Nav veriﬁes whether the navigation target is present
at the location. The robot ﬁrst performs a 360∘rotational scan to
capture a sequence of RGB images. Cosine similarities are then com-
puted between the CLIP visual embeddings of these images and the
goal’s text or visual embeddings to identify the viewing angle with the
highest semantic alignment. The selected image is subsequently fed
into GPT-4o for precise target veriﬁcation. Beyond conﬁrming target
presence, GPT-4o is also prompted to generate a sequence of
affordance-based actions to guide the robot in ﬁne-tuning its pose,
adjusting its relative position, and orientation for optimal proximity
and visibility. This ensures favorable initial conditions for downstream
manipulation tasks.

Simulation environments. Before task execution, BSC-Nav requires
environmental perception to construct preliminary landmark memory
and cognitive map representations. For simulation environments, we
implement a frontier-based52 autonomous exploration strategy for
spatial memory construction: at each time step, the system generates a
height map through depth projection to identify boundaries between
explored and unexplored regions. On traversable boundaries, the
system selects the nearest frontier point to the current position as the
next exploration target, utilizing Habitat’s scene-mesh-based greedy
navigator for low-level action planning. Upon reaching each frontier
point, the agent performs a 360-degree rotation to comprehensively
perceive the surrounding environment. This exploration process
continues until a predetermined iteration limit is reached, which is
dynamically adjusted based on scene scale. The iteration count is
deﬁned as half of the traversable area.
During task execution, both landmark memory and cognitive map
maintain continuous updates. Parameter conﬁgurations are as follows:
for landmark memory, the detector conﬁdence threshold is set to 0.55,
a standard operating point for YOLO-World on indoor datasets that
balances precision against recall for salient object retention, with a
spatial overlap distance of 1.0 m, chosen to match the navigation
success radius so that landmarks within one success zone are merged
as a single instance.
For the cognitive map, we set a voxel resolution of δ = 0.1 m3, a
grid dimension of G = 1000, and a buffer capacity of B = 10 per voxel.
The grid conﬁguration yields a 100 × 100 m2 spatial coverage that
exceeds the largest indoor scenes in HM3D and MP3D (typically < 80
m span), while Δ = 0.1 m affords object-scale spatial resolution. The
per-voxel buffer capacity B = 10 empirically balances feature diversity
against memory footprint: with 1024-dimensional features, the worst-
case working set remains within the 24 GB VRAM budget of our
deployment GPU.
For each navigation episode, the system ﬁrst retrieves candidate
target locations through the working memory module, with a max-
imum of K = 3 candidates for landmark memory retrieval and Q = 3 for
cognitive map retrieval. Our empirical analysis shows that the majority
of successful episodes locate the target at the ﬁrst explored candidate,
and beyond K, Q = 3 the cumulative success rate plateaus while path
efﬁciency (SPL) steadily declines (Supplementary Fig. 3b,c), indicating
diminishing returns in efﬁcacy at the cost of exploration efﬁciency. We
employ Stable Diffusion 3.5-Medium for cognitive map retrieval, gen-
erating three images per batch to mitigate stochasticity in visual ima-
gination. The system subsequently navigates to each candidate
location following the planned exploration sequence, performing
target veriﬁcation upon arrival at each candidate. Task success is
determined when at least one candidate passes veriﬁcation. If all
candidates fail veriﬁcation, the task is deemed unsuccessful. In simu-
lation environments, the agent operates in a discrete action space
comprising forward motion of 0.25 m and left/right turns of 30∘, with a
maximum of 5,000 steps per episode and a success distance threshold
of 1.0 m. The effective depth sensing range is [0.1, 10.0] m. These
parameter settings follow the choices made by prior works during
benchmark evaluations.
Real-world deployment. For real-world deployment, the initial phase
of spatial memory construction relies on manual teleoperation, guided
by two primary considerations. Firstly, strict safety constraints
necessitate human oversight; unlike simulated environments, auton-
omous exploration (e.g., frontier-based methods) often struggles to
navigate complex physical hazards safely in unconstrained settings.
Given our physical environment features stairs, numerous glass doors
and walls, and frequent human activity, manual guidance mitigates
signiﬁcant risks to both the robot and its payload. Secondly, employing
manual exploration isolates our core contribution, i.e., the structured
organization and retrieval of spatial memory, from the inherent failure
rates of underlying exploration algorithms. During this pre-collection
phase, an operator guides the robot through the environment at a
walking pace, while the perception pipeline records RGB-D observa-
tions and builds the cognitive map and landmark memory in real time.
During the subsequent task execution phase, BSC-Nav operates
autonomously. Since a simulator mesh does not bound the physical
environment, several adaptations are required relative to the simula-
tion conﬁguration as follows.
The robot relies on LiDAR-based SLAM for real-time localization
within a pre-built 2D occupancy grid map. At the start of each
experiment, we must manually specify the robot’s initial pose by
providing approximate pixel coordinates (x, y, θ) on the occupancy
grid map. These pixel coordinates are converted to metric map coor-
dinates via the map’s resolution and origin parameters and published
to the SLAM system as the initial pose estimate.
Several parameter adjustments are adopted to accommodate the
physical sensing conditions. The detection conﬁdence threshold is
lowered to 0.4 to compensate for increased visual noise, motion blur,
and lighting variability in uncontrolled environments, and the pre-
deﬁned landmark category set is reduced from 21 to 16. The voxel grid
is conﬁgured with Δ = 0.2 m and G = 2000. The robot executes con-
tinuous velocity commands at a nominal linear speed of 0.2 m/s and an
angular speed of 0.3 rad/s. A goal is considered reached when the
agent’s position remains within a 0.5 m threshold for a stability dura-
tion of 5.0 s, with a per-goal navigation timeout of 300 s. The com-

We identify several failure modes observed during physical
deployment: (i) Impassable regions. The local planner inﬂates obsta-
cles on the occupancy grid by a safety margin to prevent collisions. In
narrow passages or cluttered areas, the inﬂated costmap from the
SLAM system may mark the entire passageway as non-traversable,
causing the robot to become stuck or to abort the navigation goal due
to a timeout. In such cases, the system proceeds to the next candidate
location. If no candidates remain reachable, the episode is recorded as
a failure. (ii) Calibration drift. Inaccurate initial pose calibration or
accumulated SLAM drift over long trajectories can cause a systematic
offset between the robot’s estimated position and the true coordinates
stored in spatial memory. This misalignment degrades both candidate
retrieval accuracy and goal veriﬁcation, as the robot may arrive at a
location displaced from the intended memory coordinate. (iii)
Dynamic environmental disturbances. Large-scale environmental
changes between memory construction and task execution, such as
rearranged furniture, newly placed obstacles, or dense pedestrian
trafﬁc, can invalidate stored spatial memory and obstruct planned
paths. When a navigation goal is persistently unreachable due to
dynamic obstacles (e.g., sustained pedestrian ﬂow blocking a corri-
dor), the system times out, and the episode requires manual inter-
vention to re-initialize the robot’s position and re-attempt the task.

Baseline methods and comparison. End-to-end navigation methods
leverage deep neural networks to directly map egocentric observa-
tions to action sequences, implicitly encoding spatial-geometric priors
and semantic knowledge within network parameters. While they
streamline the decision-making process, they must simultaneously
learn spatial memory tracking and action planning, necessitating
extensive trajectory data or expert demonstrations for effective
training. For instance, PixNav 11 performs greedy navigation by pre-
dicting optimal actions toward salient pixels in the current view.
DAgRL 49 and PSL 50 integrate pre-trained vision-language encoders
with historical action embeddings or contrastive learning to align
visual observations with textual goals. Despite their strong perfor-
mance in simulated environments, these methods are highly suscep-
tible to data distribution shifts between
training
and testing
environments, severely impairing their generalization to real-world
physical deployments. Recent vision-language-action models
12,84,85
attempt to address these limitations by scaling model capacity and
diversifying training trajectories. Uni-NavId exempliﬁes this trend,
achieving robust performance in parsing complex multi-modal
instructions. However, these large-scale models incur signiﬁcant
computational overhead, restrict action generation frequency, and
fundamentally lack mechanisms for persistent, structured spatial
memory integration. Unlike these frameworks, BSC-Nav leverages the
explicit retrieval mechanism of its working memory to achieve a
complete decoupling of high-level semantic goal planning from low-
level physical motion control, thereby paving the way toward genuine
zero-shot, general-purpose embodied navigation.
Modular and map-based navigation methods represent a growing
class of approaches that construct explicit spatial representations to
support long-horizon planning and mitigate sim-to-real gaps. The
necessity of internal spatial representation has been recognized since
early computational models, such as the RNN-based dynamic forward
models of Tani 86, which achieved sequential planning and symbol
grounding without explicit global coordinates. However, limited by
the perceptual technologies of their era, such models could not scale
to semantically rich, large-scale scenarios. Driven by advances in
foundation models, recent methods have increasingly adopted explicit
memory structures. For example, GOAT36 and MOD-IIN54 project
closed-set semantics or visual features onto top-down maps, while
VLFM13 and UniGoal33 construct probabilistic frontier maps or abstract
graph-based representations. More recently, GridMM30 builds a top-
down dense grid memory map that aggregates ﬁne-grained visual
features via cross-modal attention (essentially modeling localized
route knowledge), whereas ETPNav32 dynamically evolves sparse
topological nodes in continuous environments (leaning towards a
reasoning-capable landmark representation).
While these methods advance beyond purely reactive processing,
they predominantly suffer from incomplete spatial memory by relying
on a single-granularity representation, either purely dense grids or
purely sparse topologies. Furthermore, they heavily depend on task-
speciﬁcally trained cross-modal Transformers or policies for memory
attention and planning, which inherently constrain their generalization
to unseen environments. BSC-Nav fundamentally distinguishes itself in
both representational granularity and retrieval paradigms. It oper-
ationalizes a brain-inspired tripartite decomposition, preventing the
semantic blurring typical of monolithic integration. Moreover, by
introducing a transparent working memory interface equipped with
visual imagination and MLLM-based logical reasoning, BSC-Nav replaces
black-box policy attention with explicit, zero-shot inference. This allows
the agent to ﬂexibly align complex semantic intents with accumulated
spatial structures without relying on heavy instruction-level ﬁne-tuning,
achieving robust spatial cognition across tasks and environments.
